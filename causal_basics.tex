\textbf{Abstract.} This note reviews potential outcomes, causal estimands, and identification by standardization and inverse propensity weighting. It distinguishes causal effects from observed associations and explains the assumptions needed to connect them. The research focus is inference with a given causal directed acyclic graph (DAG), especially when confounders are unmeasured and proxies are available; causal discovery is outside the scope of this note.

\section{Introduction}
Causal inference seeks to define, identify, and estimate the effect of an intervention on an outcome. Randomized trials provide a benchmark because treatment assignment can ensure exchangeability, but ethical, financial, or practical constraints may prevent their use. 

Observational studies are often more feasible, yet treatment assignment may depend on factors that also affect potential outcomes. Causal claims from such data therefore require assumptions supported by subject-matter knowledge. Potential outcomes, causal diagrams, and structural causal models provide complementary ways to express these assumptions.

Three common randomized designs are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Completely randomized design}: exactly $n_1$ of $n$ individuals are selected uniformly at random to receive treatment.
    \item \textbf{Bernoulli design}: individuals receive treatment independently with probability $p\in(0,1)$; the treated sample size is random.
    \item \textbf{Matched pair design}: form pairs and randomly assign one individual in each pair to treatment and the other to control.
\end{itemize}


Under marginal exchangeability, $Y(a)\indep A$, consistency and $P(A=a)>0$ imply
\[
 E[Y\mid A=a]=E[Y(a)\mid A=a]=E[Y(a)].
\]
This is an equality of population quantities. Randomization does not guarantee exact covariate balance or equality of sample means in a realized experiment. Assignment indicators can also be dependent across individuals under completely randomized and matched pair designs.

In a \emph{conditionally randomized} design, treatment probabilities may vary with a baseline covariate $Z$. Randomization within strata gives $Y(a)\indep A\mid Z$, but marginal exchangeability need not hold. This motivates standardization and weighting across strata. Exchangeability concerns $A$ and the \emph{potential} outcomes; it does not require $A\indep Y$.

\section{Potential Outcomes and Causal Estimands}
\label{sec:potential-outcomes}

Let $A$ denote treatment, $Y$ the observed outcome, and $Z$ baseline covariates. Write $Y_i(a)$ for individual $i$'s potential outcome under a specified intervention setting treatment to $a$. Expectations below refer to the target population \cite{Rubin1974EstimatingCE}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Binary treatment}: The average treatment effect (ATE) is
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ate}
        \operatorname{ATE} = E\big[Y(1)-Y(0)\big].
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Continuous treatment}: The \emph{dose-response function} is
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dose-response}
        \mu(a) = E\big[Y(a)\big].
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Comparing two outcomes for the same person]
The individual treatment effect (ITE), $Y_i(a)-Y_i(a')$, compares what would happen to the \emph{same person} under two treatment levels. For binary treatment, this is $Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)$. We usually cannot observe both outcomes for one person, so we cannot directly calculate that person's treatment effect from the observed data.
\end{remark}

A fundamental challenge in causal inference is the \emph{counterfactual} (the \emph{what if}, missing data problem). For each individual, we only observe the potential outcome corresponding to the intervention actually received; the alternative intervention remains unobserved.


\subsection{Common causal effect measures and identification}
For a binary outcome, let $p_a=P\{Y(a)=1\}$. Common causal effect measures are
\[
 \mathrm{RD}=p_1-p_0,\qquad
 \mathrm{RR}=\frac{p_1}{p_0},\qquad
 \mathrm{OR}=\frac{p_1/(1-p_1)}{p_0/(1-p_0)},
\]
when the denominators are nonzero and the odds are finite. Their null values are $0$, $1$, and $1$, respectively. The interpretation of the sign of the risk difference depends on whether $Y=1$ is beneficial or harmful.

% The \emph{sharp causal null}, $Y_i(1)=Y_i(0)$ for every individual, implies a zero ATE. The converse is false: positive and negative individual effects may cancel. For example, the lecture's Table 1.1 has equal risks $p_1=p_0=10/20$, although 12 of the 20 individuals have nonzero individual effects.

\begin{definition}[Identification versus estimation]
A causal estimand is \emph{identifiable} under a model if its value is uniquely determined by the \emph{observed-data distribution} under the assumed model. Identification can be done by two approaches:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Standardization}
    \item \textbf{Inverse propensity weighting}
\end{enumerate}


\emph{Estimation} uses a finite sample to approximate the identified functional. More data alone cannot resolve nonidentification under the assumed model.
\end{definition}



\subsection{Factual and Potential Outcomes in an SCM}

Suppose $Z$ is a baseline variable unaffected by treatment, and the factual outcome is generated by the structural equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:factual-outcome}
    y_i = \mu_3(a_i,z_i,\theta_3) + \sigma_3 e_{yi}.
\end{equation}
Under the structural causal model (SCM), setting the treatment to $a$
 gives the potential outcome
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:potential-outcome}
    Y_i(a) = \mu_3(a,z_i,\theta_3) + \sigma_3 e_{yi}.
\end{equation}
The same individual-level exogenous error is held fixed under the
 intervention. Thus,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:consistency}
    \underbrace{y_i}_{\textcolor{red}{\text{observation}}}
    = \underbrace{Y_i(a_i)}_{\textcolor{blue}{\text{causal quantity}}},
\end{equation}
which satisfies \emph{consistency}.

\begin{remark}[Factual versus causal quantities]
$Y$ records what actually happened under the treatment a person received. $Y(a)$ asks what would happen if we set that person's treatment to $a$. For example, $E[Y\mid A=1]$ is the average outcome among people who actually received treatment, while $E[Y(1)]$ is the average outcome if everyone in the target population received treatment. The first describes an observed group; the second describes an intervention. They need not be equal because the treated group may differ from the rest of the population.
\end{remark}

The central question is:
\begin{quote}
\textcolor{red}{How can we express a causal estimand as a functional of
 the observational distribution?}
\end{quote}
That is, how can we make causal claims from observational data?

\section{Linking Causal Estimands to Observational Data: Adjustment Formula and Inverse Propensity Weighting}
\label{sec:identification}

To identify causal means from observational data, assume consistency, conditional exchangeability, and positivity. Under these conditions, the adjustment formula and IPW express the same causal estimand as functionals of the observed-data distribution. Estimation is a separate step.
For the identification assumptions and the standardization and IPW arguments below, see Chapters 1--3 of Hern{\'a}n and Robins~\cite{hernanrobins2020whatif}.

\begin{assumption}[Consistency]
The observed outcome agrees with the potential outcome at the
 observed treatment level: $Y=Y(A)$, or, equivalently, \(Y=AY(1)+(1-A)Y(0)\) for binary treatments.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Conditional exchangeability]
For each treatment level $a$ of interest,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ignorability}
    Y(a) \mathrel{\indep} A \mid Z.
\end{equation}
This is a treatment-specific (weak) exchangeability condition \cite{Hirano2004}. Joint exchangeability requires $\{Y(a):a\in\mathcal A\}\indep A\mid Z$. Treatment-specific exchangeability suffices to identify each marginal mean $E[Y(a)]$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Positivity or overlap]
For binary treatment,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:binary-positivity}
    0 < P(A=1\mid Z=z) < 1.
\end{equation}
For continuous treatment, the corresponding density condition is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:continuous-positivity}
    f_{A\mid Z}(a\mid z)>0
\end{equation}
for the treatment levels of interest and almost every $z$ in the
 target population.
\end{assumption}

Positivity + Conditional exchangability is often called \emph{ignorability}. In the binary-treatment formulation of Rosenbaum and Rubin, \emph{strong ignorability} combines joint exchangeability with positivity \cite{rosenbaum1983central}.

\begin{remark}[Continuous treatment]
For a continuous treatment, such as a dose that can take any value in an interval, the probability of receiving one \emph{exact} dose is zero: $P(A=a\mid Z=z)=0$. This is normal and does not by itself mean that overlap fails. Instead, we ask whether people with covariates $z$ can receive doses near $a$; the density condition $f_{A\mid Z}(a\mid z)>0$ expresses this local availability. There is also a mathematical detail: observed data do not determine the mean response at every single exact dose without further assumptions. We typically assume that nearby doses have similar mean outcomes, for example by assuming continuity of the conditional mean in $a$.
\end{remark}

Under these assumptions, two equivalent identification strategies for binary-treatment average effects are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$g$-formula (standardization)}: average the \emph{outcome regression} $E[Y\mid Z,A]$ over the target covariate distribution.
    \item \textbf{Inverse propensity weighting}: reweight outcomes using the \emph{propensity score} $P(A=1\mid Z)$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}
    The goal of adjustment is to compare treated and untreated people who are similar in the baseline factors needed to remove confounding. If an important factor is unmeasured, differences in outcomes may still reflect that factor rather than treatment. This is why conditional exchangeability is also called ``no unmeasured confounding.'' The observed data alone generally cannot tell us whether this assumption is true.

Including more variables is not automatically better. For example, adjusting for a \emph{collider}, a variable caused by two other variables, can create a misleading association by comparing people with the same value of that variable. Adjusting for a \emph{mediator}, a variable through which treatment affects the outcome, can remove part of the effect we want to study. We therefore use knowledge of the problem and the assumed causal graph to decide which variables to adjust for.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Interpreting the identification assumptions}
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Consistency and treatment versions.} The intervention must be sufficiently well defined and match the estimand. If $A=1$ combines several drug doses with different effects, $Y(1)$ is ambiguous unless the intervention specifies a dose or a policy for choosing it. Assignment to treatment and treatment actually received also define different interventions.
 \item \textbf{SUTVA and interference.} The usual SUTVA formulation combines no relevant hidden treatment versions with no interference. Without no interference, potential outcomes may depend on the entire assignment vector: $Y_i(\mathbf a)$. The notation $Y_i(a_i)$ assumes that changing other individuals' assignments does not change individual $i$'s outcome.
 \item \textbf{Positivity.} Both treatment levels must be possible in every covariate stratum needed for exchangeability and represented in the target population. Deterministic assignment violates population positivity. Very small probabilities create practical overlap problems and unstable weights. An empty cell in a finite sample can signal poor overlap, but does not by itself prove a population probability is zero.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$g$}{g}-formula and standardization}
Under these assumptions,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:adjustment-derivation}
    \underbrace{E\big[Y(a)\big]}_{\text{causal}}
    &= E_Z\!\left[E\big[Y(a)\mid Z\big]\right] \notag\\
    &= E_Z\!\left[E\big[Y(a)\mid Z,A=a\big]\right] \notag\\
    &= E_Z\!\left[E\big[Y\mid Z,A=a\big]\right] \notag\\
    &= \int \underbrace{E\big[Y\mid Z=z,A=a\big]}_{\text{observational}}\,dF_Z(z).
\end{align}
The first equality uses iterated expectation, the second conditional exchangeability, and the third consistency. Positivity ensures that the required conditional means are supported by observed treatment groups throughout the target covariate distribution.
The conditional mean $E[Y\mid Z=z,A=a]$ is an observational quantity.

$Z$ is the \emph{adjustment set}. The resulting expression,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:g-formula}
    \boxed{\mu(a)=\int E\big[Y\mid A=a,Z=z\big]\,dF_Z(z)},
\end{equation}
is called the \emph{adjustment formula} or \emph{$g$-formula}. An estimator ($g-$estimator or outcome-regression estimator) is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:g-est}
    \boxed{\hat{\mu}_{\text{OR}}(a)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n m(a,Z_i)}
\end{equation}
where $m(a,z)$ is a regression function for $E\big[Y\mid A=a,Z=z\big]$.

\begin{remark}[Consistency of $\hat{\mu}_{\text{OR}}(a)$]
    By LLN, $\hat{\mu}_{\text{OR}}(a)$ converges to $\bar{m}$ where $\bar{m}$ is the limit of the regression function $m$. Therefore, the consistency of $\hat{\mu}_{\text{OR}}(a)$ relies on correct specification of $m$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Scope of the research]
We start with a given causal graph: we assume that the directions of the relevant causal relationships have already been specified. We also take the required adjustment variables as given. Our research asks how to learn the treatment effect when some of those variables are unmeasured. Choosing a graph or an adjustment set is a separate task. One graphical tool for choosing an adjustment set is the \emph{back-door criterion}, which checks whether adjustment blocks paths that create confounding without blocking the causal effect of interest \cite{Pearl2009}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Inverse propensity weighting}
IPW recovers the target covariate distribution by \emph{reweighting observed outcomes} to create pseudo-population. Below analysis is built upon the identification conditions. For a discrete treatment level $a$ and discrete baseline covariates $Z$, compare the standardized causal mean with the observed mean in treatment group $a$.
\[
E[Y(a)] = \underbrace{\sum_z E[Y|A=a,Z=z]\textcolor{blue}{P(Z=z)}}_{g-\text{formula}}
\]
where $E[Y|A=a,Z=z]$ is called \emph{outcome regression}.
The corresponding associational quantity is, by iterated expectation,
\[
E[Y\mid A=a] = E\!\left[E[Y\mid A=a,Z]\mid A=a\right] = \sum_z E[Y\mid A=a,Z=z]\textcolor{blue}{P(Z=z\mid A=a)}.
\]
The outer conditioning in this decomposition is $A=a$, so the identity applies to either treatment level.

Can we find a weight $f(a,z)$ such that
\[
\sum_z E[Y|A=a,Z=z]\textcolor{blue}{P(Z=z|A=a)}\textcolor{red}{f(a,z)} = \underbrace{\sum_z E[Y|A=a,Z=z]\textcolor{blue}{P(Z=z)}}_{g-\text{formula}}?
\]
By Bayes' rule, choosing
\[
\boxed{
f(a,z) = \frac{P(Z=z)}{P(Z=z|A=a)} = \frac{P(A=a)}{P(A=a|Z=z)}}
\]
gives the desired result. Therefore, the expected potential outcome can be expressed as
\begin{align*}
   E[Y(a)] &= \sum_z E[Y|A=a,Z=z]\textcolor{blue}{P(Z=z|A=a)}\textcolor{red}{\frac{P(A=a)}{P(A=a|Z=z)}} \\
   &= \sum_z E\left[Y\frac{P(A=a)}{P(A=a|Z=z)}|A=a,Z=z\right]{P(Z=z|A=a)} \\
   &= E_Z\left\{E\left[Y\frac{P(A=a)}{P(A=a|Z=z)}|A=a,Z\right]|A=a\right\} \\
   &= E\left[Y\frac{P(A=a)}{P(A=a|Z)}|A=a\right]
\end{align*}
Moreover,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weight}
    \boxed{
E[Y(a)] = \underbrace{E\left[Y \frac{P(A=a)}{P(A=a|Z)}|A=a\right]}_{E \text{ over } Y,Z|A} = \underbrace{E\left[Y \frac{I(A=a)}{P(A=a|Z)}\right]}_{E \text{ over } Y,Z,A},}
\end{equation}
which depends only on the observed-data distribution. These indicator-weight identities apply to discrete treatments with positive conditional treatment probabilities.
\begin{remark}
    The treated and untreated groups may contain different kinds of people. For example, one group may contain a larger proportion of people with a particular baseline characteristic. This difference is called \emph{covariate imbalance}.

Standardization averages both groups' predicted outcomes over the same target mix of covariates. IPW uses weights to make each treatment group represent that target mix. Both recover causal means under the stated assumptions. A difference in covariates does not necessarily create bias if it is unrelated to potential outcomes. Also, making the groups look similar in measured variables does not guarantee that they are similar in unmeasured confounders.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Association is not causality]
    From the above analysis, we can see that the difference between $E[Y|A=a]$ and $E[Y(a)]$ lies in the weighting of the outcome regression. In $E[Y|A=a]$, the outcome regression is weighted over $P(Z=z|A=a)$; while in $E[Y(a)]$, it is weighted over $P(Z=z)$. For binary treatment, if $P(Z=z|A=1) \neq P(Z=z|A=0) \neq P(Z=z)$, then such covariate imbalance causes $E[Y|A=a] \neq E[Y(a)]$ in population. $Y_i \frac{I(A_i=a)}{P(A_i=a|Z_i)}$ creates pseudo-population, where association is causality. For example, for $a=1$, outcomes are weighted by $1/P(A_i=1|Z_i)$. Of course, $P(A_i=1|Z_i)$ should be estimated (e.g., by logistic regression).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    Both identification by standardization and IWP are based on the $g-$formula, which is built upon the identification conditions.
\end{remark}


Now specialize to binary treatment.
\begin{definition}[Propensity Score]
    The propensity score \cite{rosenbaum1983central} is defined as
    \[
    e(z) = P(A=1|Z=z),
    \]
    which may be estimated by logistic regression or another suitable probability model.
\end{definition}

The propensity score is a \emph{balancing score}: $A\indep Z\mid e(Z)$. Under conditional exchangeability given $Z$, exchangeability also holds given the true propensity score. This is a population property; fitted scores do not automatically provide exact sample balance or a correct treatment model.

Horvitz--Thompson-type estimator \cite{Horvitz01121952}, which is inspired by \eqref{eq:weight}, is defined by 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:HT}
    \boxed{
    \hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{HT}}(a) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{Y_iI(A_i=a)}{\widehat P(a|Z_i)}}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat P(a|Z_i)$ is an estimate of $P(A=a|Z=z)$.

\emph{Inverse Propensity Weighting (IPW)} estimates the ATE using
\[
\boxed{
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{IPW}}= \hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{HT}}(1) - \hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{HT}}(0)
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\left[
\frac{A_iY_i}{\widehat e(Z_i)}
-
\frac{(1-A_i)Y_i}{1-\widehat e(Z_i)}
\right].}
\]
This can be seen as a subtraction between two groups of outcomes in a pseudo-population where each sample is weighted by
\[
\boxed{
w_i=\frac{A_i}{\widehat e(Z_i)}+
\frac{1-A_i}{1-\widehat e(Z_i)}.
}
\]

With known true assignment probabilities and a suitable sampling or randomization framework, the corresponding weighted mean difference is unbiased. Replacing $e$ by an estimated score does not generally preserve exact finite-sample unbiasedness; consistency requires appropriate estimation conditions: correct specification of $e$.

\begin{remark}[Weighting and continuous doses]
IPW gives more weight to people who received a treatment that was unlikely given their covariates. If that probability is very small, one person can receive a huge weight and have a large influence on the estimate. Check whether both treatments are reasonably represented across the relevant covariate groups (\emph{overlap}) and whether weighting makes the measured covariates more similar across treatment groups (\emph{balance}). Removing observations or capping large weights can help with instability, but can also introduce bias or change which population the result describes.

The displayed IPW estimator is for binary treatment. With a continuous dose, almost surely no sampled person receives a prespecified exact dose $a$, so $I(A=a)=0$ and that formula cannot estimate the mean outcome at $a$. We instead need a method that learns across doses, such as an outcome regression or a method that combines information from nearby doses using conditional treatment densities. For example, \cite{Hirano2004} generalizes binary-treatment propensity scores to continuous treatments by conditional density, called generalized propensity score (GPS), given by $r(a,z) = f_{A|Z}(a|z)$. They show its \emph{balancing property} and consider standardization via outcome regression using $r(a,z)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
    The HT estimator is sensitive to overlap and might violate the outcome range, i.e., the estimated ATE might be outside of the outcome range (see Example 3.14 in Dr. Bong's note). The Hajek, or normalized IWP estimator, is proposed to resolve this issue
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:Hajek}
    \boxed{
    \hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{Hajek}}(a) = \left(\sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{Y_iI(A_i=a)}{\widehat P(a|Z_i)}\right) \big/ \left(\sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{I(A_i=a)}{\widehat P(a|Z_i)}\right)}
\end{equation}
    
\end{remark}


\section{Confounding: Correlation versus Causation}
\label{sec:confounding}

Suppose that $Z$ is a confounder. Without adjustment, correlation
 need not represent causation. Consider the causal graph
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,
    every node/.style={circle,draw,minimum size=8mm,inner sep=1pt}]
    \node (Z) at (0,1.5) {$Z$};
    \node (A) at (-1.3,0) {$A$};
    \node (Y) at (1.3,0) {$Y$};
    \draw[->] (Z) -- (A);
    \draw[->] (Z) -- (Y);
    \draw[->] (A) -- (Y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\paragraph{A Linear Gaussian Example.}
Consider
\begin{align}
\label{eq:gaussian-model}
    Z,e_a,e_y,e_1,e_2 &\sim N(0,1), \notag\\
    A &= Z+e_a, \notag\\
    Y &= \tau A+\beta Z+\sigma_y e_y, \notag\\
    X_1 &= Z+s e_1, \qquad X_2=Z+s e_2.
\end{align}
The covariance calculation below takes the exogenous variables
 $Z,e_a,e_y,e_1,e_2$ to be mutually independent.
% Independence is implicit in the handwritten covariance calculation.

Regressing $Y$ on $A$ alone, or minimizing $E[(Y-tA)^2]$ over $t$,
 gives the population regression coefficient
\begin{align}
\label{eq:confounding-bias}
    \tau_{\mathrm{naive}}
    &= \frac{\cov(Y,A)}{\var(A)} \notag\\
    &= \frac{\cov(\tau A+\beta Z+\sigma_y e_y,A)}{\var(A)} \notag\\
    &= \frac{\tau\var(A)+\beta\cov(A,Z)}{\var(A)} \notag\\
    &= \tau+\beta\frac{\cov(A,Z)}{\var(A)} \notag\\
    &= \tau+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\beta}_{\textcolor{red}{\text{confounding bias}}}.
\end{align}
Here $\var(A)=2$ and $\cov(A,Z)=1$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:observational-vs-causal}
    \underbrace{E[Y\mid A=a]=\left(\tau+\frac{1}{2}\beta\right)a}
      _{\text{observational mean}}
    \qquad\text{versus}\qquad
    \underbrace{E[Y(a)]=\tau a}_{\text{causal mean}}.
\end{equation}
If $Z$ is observed, adjustment removes this confounding bias under model \eqref{eq:gaussian-model}. Regressing $Y$ on both $A$ and $Z$ estimates the conditional mean in \eqref{eq:g-formula}. Allowing an intercept in the fitted model, write $\hat m(a,z)=\hat\alpha+\hat\tau a+\hat\beta z$. The standardized dose-response estimator is
\[
\hat{\mu}(a) = \widehat{E[Y(a)]} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\hat{m}(a, Z_i).
\]
One may estimate $\mu(a)-\mu(a')$ by
\[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\hat{m}(a, Z_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\hat{m}(a', Z_i) = \hat{\tau}(a-a')
\]
under this additive linear model with a constant treatment slope.

\section{The Problem of Missing Confounders}
\label{sec:missing-confounders}

\subsection{A shared identification obstacle for standardization and IPW}
Let $W$ denote the observed baseline covariates and retain $Z$ for an unmeasured confounder. Even if $Y(a)\indep A\mid(W,Z)$, it need not follow that $Y(a)\indep A\mid W$. Consequently, omitting a confounder required for exchangeability affects both standardization and IPW: \emph{their usual causal interpretations rely on the same exchangeability assumption.}

For binary treatment, define $m_a(w)=E[Y\mid A=a,W=w]$ and $q_a(w)=P(A=a\mid W=w)$. With positivity and suitable integrability, iterated expectation gives the observational identity
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:observed-adjustment-functional}
 \widetilde\mu_W(a)
 :=E_W[m_a(W)]
 =E\!\left[\frac{I(A=a)Y}{q_a(W)}\right].
\end{equation}
This equality does not require exchangeability. Without exchangeability given $W$, however, neither side is guaranteed to equal the causal mean $E[Y(a)]$. Thus, a correctly estimated outcome regression or propensity score based only on $W$ can still target the wrong quantity. Balancing $W$ does not in general balance the latent $Z$ or eliminate residual confounding. Choosing IPW instead of standardization does not resolve this identification problem.

The relevant question is whether the observed variables constitute a sufficient adjustment set. An unavailable variable does not automatically invalidate identification if another observed set already ensures exchangeability. When residual confounding remains, identification requires additional structure, such as suitable assumptions about proxies or the latent-confounder model; these assumptions must be justified separately.


Recall the adjustment formula:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:missing-confounder-formula}
    E[Y(a)]
    &= E_Z\!\left[E[Y\mid A=a,Z]\right] \notag\\
    &= \int
       \underbrace{E[Y\mid A=a,Z=z]}_{\text{outcome regression}}
       \underbrace{dF_Z(z)}_{\text{confounder distribution}}.
\end{align}
\textcolor{red}{Both components require information about $Z$.}

If $Z$ is missing, we ask whether $\tau$ can be identified from
\[
    (Y,A)
    \qquad\text{or}\qquad
    (Y,A,\underbrace{X}_{\text{proxy}}).
\]
In the example above, $X=(X_1,X_2)$ consists of noisy proxies for $Z$.
The presence of proxies alone does not guarantee identification. Additional assumptions on the proxy measurements and their relationship to the latent confounder are needed; an assumed DAG must be paired with sufficient identifying restrictions.

A reference directly related to this research direction is Miao et al.~\cite{miao2018proxy}. They show that suitable proxies can identify causal effects under additional assumptions about their causal relationships and how much information they carry about the unmeasured confounder. Their result does not mean that any two noisy proxies can be substituted for $Z$ in an ordinary adjustment regression.

\begin{remark}[Unobserved versus partially missing confounders]
Here, ``missing'' can mean three different things:
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{A missing potential outcome:} we observe what happened under the treatment a person received, but not what would have happened under the alternative treatment. This is the usual counterfactual problem, even in a randomized study.
 \item \textbf{An unmeasured confounder:} a variable needed to remove confounding was not collected. In our research setting, the latent variable $Z$ plays this role. Adjusting only for the available variables may leave confounding.
 \item \textbf{A partially missing confounder:} the variable was collected for some people but is missing for others. We also need assumptions about why its values are missing, and a method for handling those missing values.
\end{itemize}
These problems need different assumptions and methods. Our main focus here is the second one: an unmeasured confounder.
\end{remark}

For the Gaussian example, the role of the proxies can be seen directly. With one proxy $X_1=Z+s e_1$, the population coefficient of $A$ in a regression of $Y$ on $(A,X_1)$ is
\[
 \tau+\beta\frac{s^2}{1+2s^2}.
\]
With both independent-error proxies, the coefficient of $A$ in a regression on $(A,X_1,X_2)$ is
\[
 \tau+\beta\frac{s^2}{2(1+s^2)},\qquad s>0.
\]
To obtain these expressions, residualize $A=Z+e_a$ and $Z$ with respect to the included proxies. If $v$ is the residual variance of $Z$, the omitted-confounder contribution is $\beta v/(1+v)$, with $v=s^2/(1+s^2)$ for one proxy and $v=s^2/(s^2+2)$ for two. At $s=0$, a proxy measures $Z$ exactly and a nonredundant adjustment recovers $\tau$. Thus, noisy proxies can reduce residual confounding in this model while generally leaving bias. Identifying a latent-confounder model requires more than simply substituting proxies for $Z$ in an ordinary adjustment regression.

\section{Causal Inference with Models}

We refer to Chapters 12 and 13 (partially) of
\cite{hernanrobins2020whatif}. In particular, we consider
model-based standardization and inverse propensity weighted
least squares (IPW-WLS).

The nonparametric implementations of these methods may suffer
from the curse of dimensionality. For example, if $Z$ is a
$p$-dimensional binary vector, there are $2^p$ possible
covariate strata. Estimating $E[Y\mid Z=z,A=a]$ separately
within each stratum requires sufficient observations for
both treatment groups, which becomes increasingly difficult
as $p$ grows. By introducing statistical models, we can
reduce this difficulty and also accommodate continuous
covariates.

\paragraph{Marginal structural model.}
Following Chapter 12, consider the model
\[
    E[Y(a)] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 a,
\]
which is called a marginal structural model (MSM)
\cite{robins2000marginal}. For a binary treatment
$A\in\{0,1\}$, the parameter
\[
    \beta_1 = E[Y(1)]-E[Y(0)]
\]
represents the average treatment effect (ATE).

The key idea is to construct a pseudo-population in
which treatment assignment is independent of the
observed confounders $Z$. Let
\[
    e(Z)=P(A=1\mid Z)
\]
denote the propensity score. We assign each observation
the inverse propensity weight
\[
    w_i =
    \frac{A_i}{e(Z_i)}
    +\frac{1-A_i}{1-e(Z_i)}.
\]

Under consistency, conditional exchangeability
$Y(a)\perp A\mid Z$, and positivity
$0<e(Z)<1$, inverse propensity weighting identifies
the counterfactual means. In particular,
\[
    E\left[\frac{AY}{e(Z)}\right]
    =E[Y(1)],
    \qquad
    E\left[\frac{(1-A)Y}{1-e(Z)}\right]
    =E[Y(0)].
\]
Therefore, in the pseudo-population, the mean outcome
among individuals with $A=a$ equals $E[Y(a)]$.

Consequently, we may consider the associational model
\[
    E^*[Y\mid A]=\theta_0+\theta_1 A,
\]
where $E^*$ denotes expectation in the pseudo-population.
Under the assumptions above, $\theta_1=\beta_1$.
Thus, a consistent estimator of $\theta_1$ in the
pseudo-population is also a consistent estimator of
the ATE in the original population.

\paragraph{Inverse propensity weighted least squares.}
In practice, we do not need to explicitly generate a
pseudo-population. Instead, we fit the associational
model using weighted least squares:
\[
    (\hat{\theta}_0,\hat{\theta}_1)
    =
    \arg\min_{\theta_0,\theta_1}
    \sum_{i=1}^n
    \hat{w}_i
    (Y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1 A_i)^2,
\]
where
\[
    \hat{w}_i =
    \frac{A_i}{\hat{e}(Z_i)}
    +\frac{1-A_i}{1-\hat{e}(Z_i)}.
\]

Intuitively, individuals who received a treatment
that was unlikely given their covariates receive larger
weights. This balances the covariate distributions
across treatment groups and allows the weighted
regression to estimate the marginal treatment effect.

For binary treatment, the resulting estimator has the
simple form
\[
\hat{\theta}_1
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n A_i\hat w_iY_i}
     {\sum_{i=1}^n A_i\hat w_i}
-
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (1-A_i)\hat w_iY_i}
     {\sum_{i=1}^n (1-A_i)\hat w_i}.
\]
Thus, IPW-WLS estimates the ATE as the difference
between the two inverse propensity weighted group means.

\paragraph{Estimation procedure.}
The practical procedure is summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Fit a propensity score model for
    $e(Z)=P(A=1\mid Z)$, for example, logistic regression.
    \item Compute the estimated inverse propensity
    weights $\hat w_i$ for all observations.
    \item Fit the weighted linear regression of $Y$
    on an intercept and $A$. The estimated coefficient
    $\hat{\theta}_1$ is the IPW-WLS estimate of the ATE.
    \item For inference, apply the nonparametric
    bootstrap by resampling individuals with replacement.
    In each bootstrap sample, re-estimate the propensity
    scores, recompute the weights, and refit the weighted
    regression. The bootstrap estimates can then be used
    to construct confidence intervals.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Comparison with outcome regression.}
Under a model-based approach, standardization and
IPW-WLS generally use different modeling assumptions.
Outcome regression specifies a model for
$E[Y\mid A,Z]$ and averages the fitted conditional means
over the covariate distribution. In contrast, IPW-WLS
specifies a model for the propensity score $P(A=1\mid Z)$
and uses the resulting weights to estimate a marginal
structural model.

Hence, standardization relies on correct specification
of the outcome model, whereas IPW-WLS relies on correct
specification of the propensity score model, in addition
to the usual causal identification assumptions.
